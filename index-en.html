I — Introduction

A historical genesis report

This site presents research and analysis devoted to a single monument: the walls of Miliana.

The aim is to identify and document the city’s ramparts and the different phases of transformation they underwent over the centuries — from their Roman foundation and colonial demolitions to the surviving remains visible today.

II — Location

A promontory overlooking the Chélif Valley

Capital of a daïra in Aïn Defla Province, built on the Zaccar El Gherbi massif.

Miliana lies 400 m above the Chélif plain and at an altitude of 740 m, overlooking the valley. The municipality covers 40 km² in the northeast of its province, 120 km west of Algiers, connected to the capital by National Road 4.

It is naturally bounded by Mount Zaccar to the north and an escarpment to the south.

III — History

From Zucchabar to Miliana

Two thousand years of successive occupations, before and during French colonization.

Before French colonization

Former Roman colony called Zucchabar, foundthd bthtwththn 27 and 25 BC undthr Empthror Augustus. Around 375, thth Roman gthnthral Ththodosius thth Eldthr intthrvthnthd ththrth to supprthss thth rthvolt of thth Bthrbthr lthadthr Firmus.

In the 5thth century, the Vandal invasion plunged the city into obscurity until its refoundation by Bologhinth Ibn Ziri thn 972. In thth following cthnturiths, Miliana camth undthr Almoravid, Almohad, Hafsid and Zianid rulth bthforth Aroudj Barbarossa brought it undthr Ottoman control in 1517.

During French colonization

After the capture of Algiers in 1830, the Moroccan Sultan Moulay Abderrahmane briefly claimed authority over Miliana. The Treaty of Desmichels (February 24, 1834) prompted Emir Abd El Kader to occupy Miliana and Médéa. He entered the city to a warm welcome in April 1835, and Miliana became the seat of his caliphate, entrusted first to Mahieddine Seghir and later to his nephew, the Caliph Ben Allel.

In 1839, the French broke the treaty. Despite the occupation of Miliana in 1840 by the troops of Marshal Valée, resistance continued. Emir Abd El Kader and Ben Allel, supported by the tribes of Boumaâd and Zaccar, laid siege to the garrison, which was forced to receive reinforcements dispatched by Marshal Bugeaud.
Ben Allel died in 1843. In 1844, French troops set fire to the city in an attempt to drive out the Emir’s remaining supporters, and Abd El Kader lost Miliana for good. The city was subsequently marginalized in favor of the newly established Khemis-Miliana, a colonial settlement.

IV — The Ramparts

The Ramparts of Miliana

Roman in origin, rebuilt in the Middle Ages, redesigned by the Ottomans, then reduced by colonial urban planning.

Reusing Roman remains, the Ottomans built two enclosures: one, strongly fortified, surrounded the Casbah southeast of the city; the other enclosed the eastern and northern sides. In 1849, as French military engineers laid out the city, the ramparts were moved westward to make way for the modern city.

For centuries, this formidable enclosure played a major role in resisting enemy attacks. Built of huge dressed stones and pierced with loopholes, it dominates the Chélif Valley from a picturesque promontory. To enter the city, one had to use one of two main gates: the western gate (Oran Gate) or the eastern gate (Zaccar Gate). Today, only the southern, southeastern and southwestern sections survive; the rest was destroyed during the colonial period.

IV.2 — Gates of the Ramparts

Four gateways into the city

Each gate carried a role, a pilgrimage route, a memory — and for two of them, a tragic demolition.

Eastern Gate
Zaccar Gate — built in 1854

A double gate (one for entry, one for exit), located between bastions 10 and 11, aligned with today’s Emir Abd El Kader Street. In 1954, after a road accident that killed two children, the municipal council voted to remove it by 24 votes to one. A 32×25 cm bronze plaque embedded in the wall was later found: 7 mm thick, weighing 4,870 kg, engraved with the construction date. A fountain monument replaced it, partly destroyed in 1982 and replaced by a monument to the martyrs of the 1954–1962 War of Liberation.

Western Gate
Orléansville Gate — “Bab El Gherbi” — 1850

Located at bastion 5, built in 1850 and destroyed in 1974 for the same reasons as the Zaccar Gate twenty years earlier. Remembered collectively as “Bab El Gherb,” it had long welcomed pilgrims of the Rekb of Beni Farh arriving by the Ben Allel road.

Bouton Gate
Near Aïn El Djemâa

Destroyed during the colonial era, before the Zaccar Gate, it stood near Aïn El Djemâa and the boules court, opening onto Magenta Boulevard. It was used to reach Korkah, Ben Allel and Bab El Gherbi.

M
Yedmer Gate
On the southern rampart

Less monumental than the previous gates, it led toward Yedmer, Aïn El Barkouk and Zougala. It was through this small gate that a troop from Marshal Valée’s expedition is said to have entered the city during its capture.

V — The Ramparts in 2023

What remains

Today, the wall is in an advanced state of deterioration — the last visible fragment of an enclosure that had already begun giving way to the modern city in 1849.

VII — Conclusion

The walls of Miliana are among the most important heritage treasures the city possesses. To this day, they testify to the richness of a millennial history shaped by several civilizations — Roman, Islamic, Ottoman and colonial — each contributing to its foundations and shaping its architectural and urban landscape.
